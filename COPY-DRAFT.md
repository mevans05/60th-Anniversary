**Register:** warm, dry, deflecting. They're pleased to be celebrated but would

## Squarespace page

**Headline**

> # Tom & Yvonne
> ## Sixty Years
> *Son of a six and seven.*

The subhead does the personality work without explaining itself. Family gets it
instantly; everyone else finds it charming and moves on.

**Opening**

> Our parents have been married sixty years.
>
> In that time Dad has called roughly everyone he has ever met "ya buzzard," Mom's
> laugh has been independently confirmed audible from five states away, and
> between them they have worn "son of a six and seven" down to the nub.
>
> They'd rather we didn't make a fuss. We're making one anyway.
>
> Join us for dinner at The Greatful Dane.

## Judgment calls

Two pieces of the source material are excellent but are held back from the
public page. Both are the family's call, not mine — flagging the tradeoff, not
vetoing.

**"She's always going to be your mother, but she doesn't always have to be my
wife!"**
Genuinely funny and unmistakably him. But on the invitation it is a joke about
ending the marriage, printed on the announcement of its sixtieth year, read by a
guest list that includes people who will take it flatly. It also loses something
in print: it is a line he says *to his kids*, with timing. It belongs in the
toast, delivered live by one of you, or as the epigraph of the memory book.
That is a better home for it than a web page.

**"The Gimp"**
A family nickname with a backstory the family knows and much of the guest list
does not. Out of context and in print it reads differently than it does in the
kitchen. Recommend keeping it off the page and out of the form; it's at home in
the memory book or a toast, where whoever uses it can carry the context.

"Tommy Boy" has no such problem and could go on the page if wanted — e.g. the
sign-off could read *"— the children of Tommy Boy and Yvonne."*
